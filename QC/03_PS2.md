# PS2: Quantum Communication and Eavesdropping Lab

**Level:** Beginner - Moderate.

**Core idea:** Use a quantum library to build an interactive communication experiment and investigate interception and noise.

## Problem statement

Copying an ordinary digital message does not necessarily change it. Quantum communication behaves differently: measuring an unknown quantum state in an unsuitable way can disturb it.

Build an interactive application demonstrating the BB84 quantum key-distribution protocol. Alice and Bob exchange simulated qubits and compare selected information to identify a potential shared bit sequence. Eve can intercept transmissions. Users must be able to explore how interception, noise, and the amount of publicly compared data affect the results.

Use an existing quantum library for state preparation and measurement. The programming challenge is the protocol workflow, configurable experiments, visual explanations, and result analysis.

## Understanding the protocol

Alice chooses random bits and one of two encoding methods, called **bases**, for each bit. Bob independently chooses a basis for each measurement.

When their bases match, an ideal undisturbed transmission gives Bob the same bit Alice sent. When their bases differ, his result is random. They publicly compare basis choices and retain only matching positions, a process called **sifting**.

They then reveal a random sample of retained bits to estimate the error rate. Disclosed bits are removed from the remaining sequence. Disagreements indicate disturbance that may arise from interception, noise, or both.

This application ends with remaining candidate bits and a disturbance decision; it is not a complete secure key-distribution system.

## Required functionality

### Quantum transmission

Generate Alice's random bits and bases, prepare the corresponding qubit states using a quantum library, and measure each transmission in Bob's random basis.

Use the Z and X bases: two different ways of preparing and reading a qubit. Explain their role in the interface without assuming knowledge of matrix notation.

Process transmissions individually or in manageable batches. These are separate qubits, so a long message must not require one enormous joint state vector.

### Interception and noise

Implement an **intercept-and-resend** attacker. Eve intercepts a configurable fraction of transmissions, chooses a random basis, measures, and prepares a replacement using her result and basis before forwarding it to Bob.

Perform these state preparations and measurements through the quantum library. Do not represent Eve by simply inserting a fixed percentage of incorrect answers.

Separately provide a receiver-error probability that flips Bob's recorded bit after measurement. Clearly label this as a simplified classical readout-error model, not a complete quantum-channel model. The separate controls reveal configured causes of disturbance, although Alice and Bob cannot reliably identify the cause from error rate alone.

### Sifting and public comparison

Retain matching-basis positions. Randomly select a user-controlled fraction for public comparison and calculate the **quantum bit error rate**, or QBER:

```text
QBER = disagreements in the public sample / number of compared bits
```

Remove disclosed positions from the remaining candidate sequences. Let users select a demonstration threshold and report “disturbance detected,” “below the selected threshold,” or “insufficient data.” A result below the threshold must not be described as proof of security.

Handle cases with no sifted bits, no compared bits, or no remaining candidate bits without invalid percentages or misleading success messages.

### Interactive experiment view

Build a local browser-based interface with controls for transmission count, interception fraction, receiver-error probability, sample fraction, decision threshold, and random seed.

Show preparation, transmission, measurement, sifting, public comparison, and remaining candidate bits as distinct stages. For selected transmissions, show Alice's bit and basis, Eve's action, Bob's basis and result, and whether the position was retained or disclosed.

Distinguish an **educational view**, exposing simulated internal information, from the **public transcript**, containing basis announcements and disclosed sample bits. Internal data must not appear to be information automatically available to every participant.

### Repeated experiments and analysis

Run batches for an ideal channel, interception without receiver errors, and receiver errors without interception. Plot measured error rates and remaining candidate lengths across runs. Show how sample size affects the stability of the error estimate.

With independent random bases and full intercept-and-resend without additional errors, the expected error rate among sifted bits is approximately 25% over many transmissions. Individual samples fluctuate and may fail to reveal disturbance, especially when little data is compared.

Export settings and results as JSON or CSV. Include the seed and dependency versions so experiments can be reproduced and compared.

## Implementation boundaries

Use Python with Qiskit or another documented quantum SDK for preparation and measurement. Run locally; quantum hardware, cloud credentials, and paid services are not required. Choose an interface framework suitable for the application.

Assume an authenticated public classical channel for basis and sample announcements. Authentication, error correction, privacy amplification, real networking, and message encryption are outside scope. Remaining candidate bits may disagree or be partially known to Eve; do not label them a finished secure key.

- **A detailed README** explaining BB84, participant roles, basis selection, sifting, sample disclosure, QBER, and the noise model in beginner-friendly language. Include installation instructions, exact dependencies, launch and usage instructions, screenshots or actual outputs, and reproducible examples of the three scenarios. Explain finite-sample uncertainty, why errors do not uniquely identify an attacker, what the public transcript reveals, and why remaining bits are not yet a secure key. Credit the resources used.
